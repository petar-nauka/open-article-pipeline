# Genre decisions

Choose the structure that best answers the reader's question. These are cues, not mandatory outlines.

| Genre | Reader need | Common evidence risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| News | What happened, when, who is affected, what follows | Announcement mistaken for completion; old status presented as new |
| Explainer | How something works and why it matters | Analogy overstated; exceptions omitted |
| Analysis | What evidence implies and where uncertainty remains | Interpretation presented as a sourced fact |
| Interview or Q&A | What the person actually said and in what context | Reconstructed quotation or invented question |
| Profile or feature | A supported account of a person, project, or place | Invented scene, motivation, or narrative detail |
| Guide | What a reader can do and under which conditions | Advice generalized beyond evidence or local rules |
| Review or comparison | How options differ under stated criteria | Missing denominator, criteria changed midstream, undisclosed interest |
| Opinion | A clearly attributed argument built on checkable premises | Factual premise treated as exempt from verification |

Do not turn every subsection into the same question format. Choose a headline that reflects the article's actual support and a lead that helps the intended audience decide whether to read on. A useful article may be shorter than the requested target if the evidence is limited.
